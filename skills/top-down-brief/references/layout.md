# Layout: points or prose

The pyramid decides the order of ideas. Layout decides how the reader's eye finds them. A busy reader scans before they read: if a piece announces three things and then explains them in a paragraph, the reader has to dig the three things out. Lay them out instead.

**The rule:** the body shows the same structure as the skeleton. If the skeleton has a list, the body has that list. The Structure summary after a draft is a check on the body, not a substitute for it.

## Always use points for

1. **An announced set.** Any sentence that announces a number or a set ("three things", "two conditions", "the changes are", "it requires", "as follows") is followed directly by one point per item. Never announce a list and then write it as a paragraph.
2. **Three or more parallel items.** Requirements, conditions, risks, options, changes, deliverables, dependencies, documents, people or dates of the same kind: three or more go on their own lines, even when no sentence announces them.
3. **Steps in an order.** Anything the reader must do or follow in sequence is numbered.
4. **The ask.** Each action the reader must take is its own numbered line, with the owner and the date.
5. **Figures to compare.** Prices, ranges, tiers or options compared on the same measure go on separate lines, or in a small table if there are two or more measures.

## Group long lists

Six or more items at the same level are too many to take in as one list. Group them into two to five groups (by owner, phase, place or theme), give each group a bold sub-heading or one-sentence lead-in, and list its items beneath. A single flat list of nine actions is a pile, not a structure. The exception is a short dated checklist the reader will tick off in order, where date order is the point; even then, lead with the item that everything else depends on.

## Keep as prose

- **The governing thought** and the one-line context before it.
- **A chain of reasoning**, where each sentence depends on the one before ("because", "so", "therefore"). Breaking a deductive chain into bullets breaks the logic.
- **One or two short items.** Join two items with "and" in one sentence ("The only changes are the updated cash forecast and the corrected headcount on page 4."). Never turn one or two short items into bullets, and never add a heading to a message that has no key line.
- **Bad news and sensitive messages**, where bullets read as cold. State the point in prose; list only the practical next steps.
- **A short reply** of two or three sentences.

## How to write the points

- **Lead-in sentence first.** Under each key-line point, write one sentence that states the point, then the points that support it. The lead-in is a statement ("The platform needs three changes before go-live, and the first may be substantial."), not a label.
- **One idea per point.** If a point needs a second sentence, keep it to one more. If it needs three, it is a sub-section: give it a bold lead-in and a short paragraph.
- **Start with the substance.** Put the thing first, then the detail: "**Split the release function:** today it runs with freeze and burn, so those would run unattended too." A bold lead-in of two to five words helps a scanning reader.
- **Parallel form.** All points in one list start the same way (all verbs, or all nouns) and are the same kind of idea.
- **Numbers or bullets.** Number points when order, count or reference matters ("see point 2"), and for asks. Otherwise use bullets.
- **One level only in email.** No nested bullets in an email. If a point needs its own sub-points, it should be a bold sub-heading with a short list under it.
- **Plain characters that survive pasting.** Use `-` or `1.` at the start of a line and `**bold**` for lead-ins. These carry into Outlook and Gmail; tables may not, so prefer lines over tables in email.

## Paragraph limits

- Email and letters: at most three sentences, or about 60 words, per paragraph. Anything longer is either two ideas (split it) or a list in disguise (lay it out).
- Memos and reports: at most four or five sentences per paragraph under a heading.

## Example

**Before** (announces three items, then buries them in a paragraph):

> Moving to the new payroll provider needs three changes on our side, and the first may be substantial. Our HR system currently sends all employee changes in one nightly file, but the provider sets approval rules per file, not per change type, so if salary changes stay in the same file as address changes, they would be approved without review too, and our developers will need to split them out. Separately, every pay-related form must move to the provider's templates, not just the payslip, and we must reconcile each pay run ourselves since the provider does not send a confirmation for off-cycle payments.

**After:**

> Moving to the new payroll provider needs three changes on our side, and the first may be substantial.
>
> 1. **Separate salary changes from other employee updates.** Today they go out in one nightly file, and the provider approves per file, not per change type. Left together, salary changes would pass without review. Our developers will need to split them; I'll get scope and cost before we commit.
> 2. **Move every pay-related form to the provider's templates**, not just the payslip.
> 3. **Reconcile each pay run ourselves**, because the provider sends no confirmation for off-cycle payments.

## In Review mode

Count the announced sets and the runs of three or more parallel items in the draft. Each one written as a paragraph is a **Layout** fix: quote it in "What's getting in the way" and lay it out in the revised version.
